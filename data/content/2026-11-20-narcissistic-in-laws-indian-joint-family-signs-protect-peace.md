**Narcissistic in-laws often show a pattern of needing constant admiration, controlling decisions, dismissing your feelings, and blaming others when things go wrong. In an Indian joint family, this can be hard to name because it is easily mistaken for "family culture" or "elders' way." You can protect your peace by recognising the pattern, setting calm limits, and building support outside the home.**

## What are the signs of narcissistic in-laws?

"Narcissistic" is a word people use for a pattern of behaviour. Only a trained professional can assess whether someone has a personality disorder, and that needs a proper meeting with the person. As a family member, you can still notice patterns that affect you.

Common signs include:

- They need to be the centre of attention, and every event must go their way.
- Your achievements are ignored, or quietly turned into their credit.
- Your feelings are called "drama" or "too sensitive."
- They compare you with other daughters-in-law or sons-in-law, or with their own child.
- Affection depends on obedience. When you disagree, they go cold or sulk for days.
- They share private things you told them, or speak about you to relatives.
- They never apologise. If anything goes wrong, someone else is at fault.
- They play the victim ("After all I have done for this family") when you set a limit.

One bad day does not make a pattern. Repeated behaviour that leaves you anxious, small, or constantly on guard deserves your attention.

## Why does it feel harder in an Indian joint family?

Joint family life has real warmth and support. It also has features that can make unhealthy behaviour hard to challenge.

Respect for elders is a deep value. Many of us learn early that questioning a parent or in-law is disrespectful. Daughters-in-law often carry extra pressure to adjust, and sons-in-law may be expected to stay quiet. Living in shared space means little privacy and few exits. There may be no place to cool down after a hurtful remark.

"Log kya kahenge" adds another layer. You may fear that speaking up will damage your spouse's standing or your own. For Indians abroad, distance can bring guilt on top of the tension, especially when visits, money, or caregiving become a source of pressure.

None of this means your family is bad. It means the setting can hide a pattern that would be easier to see elsewhere.

## How do you protect your peace without a fight?

You cannot control another person's behaviour. You can decide how you respond. Small, steady steps usually work better than one big confrontation.

- **Keep your replies short and calm.** "I will think about it" or "That does not work for me" needs no long explanation. Long explanations give more material to argue with.
- **Decide what you will share.** You do not owe every detail of your finances, health, or marriage to the whole household.
- **Do not react to every provocation.** Some remarks are meant to pull you into a quarrel. You may choose not to take the bait.
- **Create small pockets of privacy.** A walk, a call with a friend, a closed door for twenty minutes, or time with a journal can help.
- **Keep your own routines.** Sleep, meals, movement, and friendships are easy to lose in a tense house. They are worth protecting.
- **Stay polite, not pliable.** You can speak respectfully and still say no.

Expect some pushback at first. People used to a certain arrangement often resist change, even a gentle one.

## What if your spouse does not see the problem?

This is very common. Your spouse grew up inside this family and may have learned to accept the behaviour as normal. They may feel caught between you and their parents, and might respond with defensiveness.

Choose a calm moment, not the heat of an argument. Describe specific incidents and how they affected you, instead of labelling their parent. Say, "When this happened, I felt dismissed," rather than "Your mother is a narcissist." Then ask for one small, clear change, such as support when a comment crosses a line.

If conversations keep going in circles, a neutral space such as couples or individual sessions can help both of you speak more openly.

## How can this affect your mind and body?

Living with ongoing criticism or control is tiring. Many people describe constant worry, poor sleep, irritability, low mood, or a sense of walking on eggshells. Some feel unwell in their body without a clear medical reason. Doubting yourself is also common. When someone repeatedly tells you that your view is wrong, you may begin to trust your own judgement less. ## When should you seek professional support?

Consider speaking to a mental health professional if:

- You feel anxious, low, or on edge most days.
- Your sleep, appetite, or work have been affected for weeks.
- You have started doubting your own memory or judgement.
- Tension at home is straining your marriage.
- You feel isolated or unable to talk to anyone in the family.
- You are using food, alcohol, or other habits to cope.

You do not need to wait for a crisis. A therapist can help you understand what is happening, think through boundaries that fit your family, and give you a private place to be heard. ## FAQ

**Is every difficult in-law a narcissist?**

No. Many in-laws are controlling or hurtful because of stress, age, or old habits, without having a personality disorder. Only a trained professional can assess that, and only after meeting the person. What matters for you is the pattern and its effect: repeated disregard, control, and blame that leave you anxious or feeling small.

**Can I set boundaries without disrespecting elders?**

Yes. Boundaries can be polite and calm. You are not refusing respect. You are deciding what you will discuss, share, or agree to. Use short, steady sentences such as "I will think about it," and repeat them if needed. Respect and limits can exist together, even if the family takes time to accept this.

**What if my spouse does not see the problem?**

This is common, as your spouse may have grown up treating the behaviour as normal. Choose a calm moment, describe specific incidents and how they affected you, and avoid attacking their parents. Ask for one small change together. If talks keep stalling, couples or individual sessions can give you both a neutral space.

**Can therapy help if I cannot move out?**

Yes, therapy can support you even when living arrangements stay the same. A therapist can help you understand the pattern, think through calmer ways to respond, and look after your sleep and mood. It does not change your in-laws, and no outcome can be promised. It gives you a steady place to bring your own concerns.
